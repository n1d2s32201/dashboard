Human Rights Council · United Kingdom · Agenda: De-criminalizing Homelessness and Ensuring Human Rights Protection

UK Position

Core position: the United Kingdom believes that no one should be punished simply for being homeless. Support, housing and prevention are more effective and more rights-respecting than fines or prosecution, while public order and safety for everyone must still be protected.
  • Homelessness is a housing and welfare problem first, not a criminal one.
  • Prevent homelessness early (housing, benefits, health, leaving care and prison).
  • Protect the dignity, privacy and safety of people sleeping rough.
  • Treat all groups equally, including women, young people, migrants and people with disabilities.
  • Share good practice and support other States through the Council.

Useful distinction: these are sensible debate positions consistent with the UK's stated direction. Do not present every bullet as a quotation from UK policy, and note that the UK is made up of separate legal systems (England and Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland).

Main arguments

1. Criminal penalties do not solve homelessness
Fines and criminal records can push people further from housing and work. The Vagrancy Act 1824 criminalised rough sleeping and begging in England and Wales; Parliament voted to repeal it in 2022, and the Government has since said it intends to complete repeal (verify current status).
2. Housing is a human right
The right to an adequate standard of living including housing appears in UDHR Art. 25 and ICESCR Art. 11. Criminalising a basic life necessity raises dignity, privacy and equality concerns.
3. Prevention and support work
The Homelessness Reduction Act 2017 placed duties on councils to prevent and relieve homelessness. Finland's Housing First model is often cited internationally.
4. Public order still matters
Governments can address genuinely harmful behaviour (violence, harassment) without criminalising the status of being homeless. Be ready to answer concerns from business owners and residents.

Art. 1All human beings are born free and equal in dignity and rights.Dignity of people sleeping rough.
Art. 7Equality before the law and equal protection.Laws that target a status.
Art. 9No arbitrary arrest or detention.Arrests for sleeping outdoors.
Art. 12No arbitrary interference with privacy or home.Removal of belongings and tents.
Art. 13Freedom of movement and residence.Move-on orders and banishment.
Art. 25Adequate standard of living including food, clothing, housing and medical care.The core housing right.

Binding instruments and bodies

  • ICESCR: Art. 11 (adequate standard of living including housing). UK is a State Party; see also CESCR General Comment No. 4 (1991) on adequate housing and No. 7 (1997) on forced evictions.
  • ICCPR (in force 1976): Art. 7 (cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment), Art. 9, Art. 12, Art. 17 (privacy), Art. 26 (non-discrimination).
  • ECHR: Art. 3, Art. 8 and Art. 14. In Lăcătuş v Switzerland (January 2021) the European Court found that fining a homeless woman for begging violated Art. 8.
  • UN Special Rapporteur on adequate housing reports regularly on homelessness as a human rights issue.
Do not confuse: privacy is Art. 12 UDHR but Art. 17 ICCPR. The UDHR is not binding; ICESCR and ICCPR bind States Parties. Housing is a UDHR and ICESCR right, not an ICCPR one.

Useful test: any restriction should be lawful, necessary, proportionate and non-discriminatory.

Topic Explained

Homelessness
Not only sleeping rough. It includes sofa-surfing, hostels, temporary accommodation, and living in unsafe or unsuitable housing.
Criminalisation of homelessness
Using criminal or quasi-criminal law (fines, arrests, bans, move-on orders, confiscation of belongings) against behaviour linked to being homeless, such as sleeping outdoors, begging or camping.
Housing First
A model that gives people stable housing first, then offers support, instead of requiring them to meet conditions beforehand. Finland is a widely cited example (verify details).
Housing-related terms
Rough sleeping: sleeping outdoors or in places not meant for sleeping. Temporary accommodation: short-term housing provided by councils. Priority need: categories of people councils must prioritise in England and Wales.
Why criminalising is criticisedWhy some governments defend rules
  • Fines and records make housing and jobs harder.
  • It targets people for having nowhere to go.
  • It can deter people from seeking help.
  • Evidence for deterrence is weak (verify).
  • Concerns about public safety and public health.
  • Pressure from residents and businesses.
  • Needed to direct people to services in some views.
  • Shortage of shelter makes enforcement controversial.
Key argument: you cannot punish people for sleeping outdoors when there is nowhere else for them to go.

Practical safeguards you can propose

  1. Repeal or reform laws that punish homelessness as such.
  2. Prevention duties on public authorities.
  3. Housing-led approaches such as Housing First.
  4. Safe, adequate shelter.
  5. Protection from forced eviction and legal remedies.
  6. Non-discrimination and targeted help for women, youth and migrants.
  7. Training police to refer rather than penalise.
  8. Data collection and reporting.

What the Human Rights Council can do

  • 47 Member States.
  • Adopts resolutions and recommendations.
  • Can request OHCHR reports; works with Special Rapporteurs and the Universal Periodic Review.
  • Cannot force States to change domestic law.

UK Enforcement & Support

How the system works (verify current rules)

  • Support: local authorities have duties to prevent and relieve homelessness under the Homelessness Reduction Act 2017 (England).
  • Enforcement: the Vagrancy Act 1824 offences, PSPOs and other orders in England and Wales.
  • Outreach: councils and charities such as Crisis and Shelter provide outreach, advice and shelter.
  • Devolution: Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own homelessness laws and policies.

Criticisms

  • Rough sleeping and temporary accommodation have been rising in England (check latest MHCLG statistics).
  • Some people fall outside the "priority need" test.
  • PSPOs and similar powers have been used in ways critics say target homeless people.
  • Slow progress on completing repeal of the Vagrancy Act.

Safeguards the UK can point to

  • Statutory prevention duties.
  • Cross-party support for repealing the Vagrancy Act.
  • Charities and local authorities working together on outreach.

How to ask intelligent POIs

  • One sentence, a real question.
  • Target a gap: funding, enforcement, mandate, who pays.
  • Ask something that forces a commitment.
  • Stay polite.

Example POIs for this agenda

  • “If rough sleeping is no longer an offence, how does your clause address public safety concerns?”
  • “Who would pay for the shelter and housing your proposal requires?”
  • “Does the delegate accept that a criminal record can make it harder for someone to find housing?”
  • “Would your clause also stop orders that move people on without offering alternatives?”
  • “The Council cannot legislate; how would this be monitored?”

Answering POIs

  • Pause, answer briefly, bridge back to your point.
  • “Thank you for that question. The United Kingdom believes…”
  • Do not know? Say so (see Challenges & Responses).

Typical flow of a session

  1. Roll call and setting the agenda.
  2. Speakers' list and formal debate.
  3. Moderated and unmoderated caucuses.
  4. Working papers become draft resolutions.
  5. Amendments, then voting.

Before committee

  • Read the rules of procedure and ask how points work.
  • Prepare a 1-minute opening speech and 3 key points.
  • Learn which countries are likely allies or opponents.
  • Bring a short clause bank (e.g. “Requests OHCHR to prepare a report on…”).

Moderated and unmoderated caucuses

  • Moderated: speak early on a narrow topic, make one clear point, link back to previous speakers.
  • Unmoderated: move quickly. Find 2–3 delegates, offer a concrete clause, collect signatories, share paper.

Speeches

Simple structure (about 60–90 seconds)

  1. Hook: a true, striking point.
  2. Position: the UK's view.
  3. Evidence: one law or dated fact.
  4. Solution: one or two proposals.
  5. Close: a memorable line.
Sample opening speech (UK, HRC)
“Honourable Chair, distinguished delegates. A fine cannot give someone a home. The United Kingdom believes that no one should be punished simply for being homeless. In 2022 our Parliament voted to repeal the Vagrancy Act of 1824, and we continue to work on prevention duties for local authorities. We recognise that challenges remain, including rising rough sleeping. We urge the Council to encourage States to focus on housing and support instead of penalties, and to request an OHCHR report on best practice. Thank you, Chair.”
Sample reply speech in a moderated caucus
“The United Kingdom thanks the delegate of… and shares the concern about public safety. We would add that addressing harmful behaviour directly is different from punishing people for having nowhere to live. We invite delegates to support this wording.”

Delivery tips

  • Slow down; look up.
  • One idea per sentence.
  • Use dated facts, not exaggeration.

Negotiation

Approaching delegates

  • “Hello, I'm the delegate of the United Kingdom. Could we talk about ending penalties for homelessness?”
  • Ask their priority first, then find overlap.
  • Bring a one-line proposal.

Building alliances

  • Many delegates will value the right to housing, dignity and non-discrimination. Others may stress public order, sovereignty or limited budgets. Ask, don't assume.
  • Look for States with Housing First or prevention experience and for those seeking technical assistance.
  • Offer a shared clause, co-sponsorship or credit for their idea.

Where the UK can compromise

  • Flexible: wording, timelines, voluntary guidance, reporting.
  • Careful: do not commit the UK to new treaties or spending without instruction; be careful with language that implies the UK has fully ended criminalisation (check repeal status).

Example negotiation lines

  • “Could we say ‘encourages’ instead of ‘requires’? That stays within the Council's mandate.”
  • “If we add support for housing-led approaches, would your delegation co-sponsor?”

Challenges & Responses

“Isn't homelessness still criminalised in the UK?”
“Parliament voted to repeal the Vagrancy Act in 2022 and the Government has said it intends to complete repeal. We recognise that progress has taken time.” (Check the current status first and be honest if it is not yet in force.)
“Rough sleeping is rising in the UK. What are you doing?”
“We recognise the concern. Local authorities have legal duties to prevent and relieve homelessness, and we are open to learning from others.”
“Councils use orders to move homeless people on.”
“We take that concern seriously. Powers should be used proportionately and with support available, and we welcome specific suggestions.”
“Doesn't decriminalising lead to more disorder?”
“Addressing harmful behaviour such as violence or harassment is different from punishing people for having nowhere to live.”
“Who pays for housing?”
“Prevention and housing-led approaches can reduce longer-term costs, and the Council can encourage cooperation and technical assistance.” (Avoid numbers you cannot source.)
“Is housing really a human right?”
“The right to an adequate standard of living, including housing, is recognised in the Universal Declaration and in the Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights.”

Answering unexpected questions

  • Pause, restate the question, answer in one sentence, bridge back.
  • Separate what you know from what you assume.

If you do not know

  • “I do not have that figure in front of me. I will check the official source and return to it.”
  • Never invent a statistic.
