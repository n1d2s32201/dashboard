Human Rights Council · United Kingdom · Agenda: Implementing Human Rights Safeguards in the Proliferation of Mass Surveillance and Predictive Profiling Technologies

UK Position

Core position: the United Kingdom can support surveillance for legitimate public-safety and national-security purposes, but its use should be lawful, necessary, proportionate, fair and subject to safeguards.
  • Support responsible use of surveillance, not a blanket ban.
  • Protect privacy and personal data.
  • Prevent discrimination and test systems for bias.
  • Require meaningful human oversight of consequential decisions.
  • Support international cooperation and clearer standards.

Useful distinction: these are sensible debate positions consistent with the UK's stated balancing approach. Do not present every bullet as a quotation from UK policy.

Main arguments to make

1. Security and rights are not opposites
Surveillance can help prevent serious crime, but only where there is a legitimate aim and the intrusion is justified. “Security and human rights must be protected together.”
2. A prediction is not proof
A risk score or facial-recognition match should not, by itself, establish that someone committed a crime. Important decisions need reliable evidence and meaningful human review.
3. Safeguards must keep pace with technology
The UK openly accepts its legal framework for facial recognition is complex: the Home Office launched a consultation on 4 December 2025 on a clearer framework. Use this to show good faith.
4. Support others, don't lecture
Offer technical assistance, shared testing methods and training so States with fewer resources can build safeguards.

Universal Declaration of Human Rights (adopted 10 December 1948, not legally binding)

ArticleRightLink to the agenda
Art. 7Equality before the law and equal protection against discrimination.Biased algorithms treating groups differently.
Art. 9No arbitrary arrest, detention or exile.Stops based on a computer match or risk score alone.
Art. 10Fair and public hearing by an independent, impartial tribunal.Right to challenge automated decisions.
Art. 11Presumption of innocence.Predictive profiling can treat people as suspects before any act.
Art. 12No arbitrary interference with privacy, family, home or correspondence.The core privacy right.
Art. 19Freedom of opinion and expression.Chilling effect of being constantly watched.

International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (adopted 1966, in force 1976, legally binding on States Parties)

  • Article 17: privacy.
  • Article 19: freedom of expression.
  • Article 26: equality before the law and equal protection without discrimination.
  • Article 9: liberty and security of person; protection against arbitrary arrest or detention.
Don't mix them up: privacy is Art. 12 UDHR but Art. 17 ICCPR. Equality is Art. 7 UDHR but Art. 26 ICCPR.

Useful test: any restriction should be lawful, necessary, proportionate and for a legitimate aim.

Topic Explained

Mass surveillance
Monitoring large numbers of people, e.g. CCTV, phone or internet data, location information or facial recognition.
Predictive profiling
Using personal data and statistical or artificial-intelligence systems to estimate someone's behaviour, characteristics or future risk.
Artificial intelligence (AI)
Software that finds patterns in data to make predictions or classifications. Its output depends on the data it learned from, so skewed data can produce skewed results.
Algorithmic bias
When a system produces systematically less accurate or unfair outcomes for some groups.
Human oversight
A trained person meaningfully checks an automated result instead of simply accepting it.
Possible benefitsMain risks
  • Help locate suspects or missing people faster.
  • Help prevent serious crime and terrorism.
  • Efficiency: analyse more data than humans can.
  • Privacy invasion and excessive data collection.
  • False matches or inaccurate risk predictions.
  • Discrimination against particular groups.
  • Chilling effect on lawful speech or activity.
  • Data retained, shared or reused improperly.
Key argument: a prediction is not proof.

Practical safeguards you can propose

  1. Necessity and proportionality: legitimate purpose, justified intrusion.
  2. Purpose limitation: no quiet reuse for unrelated purposes.
  3. Retention limits: delete data when no longer needed.
  4. Independent testing: before deployment and regularly after.
  5. Human review: before serious decisions affecting rights.
  6. Transparency: what is used, why, what safeguards apply.
  7. Complaints and remedies: a way to challenge errors.
  8. Independent oversight: inspect compliance, investigate misuse.

What the Human Rights Council can do

  • It has 47 Member States.
  • Adopts resolutions setting out guidance and recommendations.
  • Can request reports and advice from OHCHR.
  • Can request technical assistance for States.

UK Facial Recognition

How police use it

The Home Office factsheet says police use three types: retrospective (comparing images after an event), live (scanning faces against a watchlist in real time) and operator-initiated (an officer checks an image on request).

Safeguards (existing)

  • If there is no match in live use, biometric data is deleted immediately and automatically; the watchlist is destroyed after each operation.
  • After a possible match, an officer decides what action, if any, to take. A match is not an automatic arrest.
  • Parliamentary answer, 13 April 2026: Home Office-funded police systems must be independently tested to operate at settings where bias is negligible.

Scale

  • As of November 2025, 13 police forces had used or were using live facial recognition (Government statement).

Criticisms

  • The legal basis is a patchwork (common law, statutes, codes, guidance) rather than one dedicated law.
  • Concerns about accuracy and bias across demographic groups.
  • Transparency, watchlist criteria and proportionality of use in public places.
  • Court of Appeal in Bridges (2020) found shortcomings in one force's policy.

Current developments (Proposed)

  • 4 December 2025: factsheet published and consultation launched on a clearer legal framework.
  • Consultation ran to 12 February 2026. It is closed; never call it open.
  • 13 April 2026: Government considering responses; intends to legislate in due course.
Time-stamped claims: if challenged, quote the date and source, not “always” or “currently” without a date.

How to ask intelligent POIs

  • Keep it one sentence and a real question, not a speech.
  • Target a weakness: vague wording, missing funding, no oversight, mandate overreach.
  • Ask something that makes the speaker commit, not a yes/no they can easily dodge.
  • Be polite. Never insult the delegate.

Example POIs for this agenda

  • “Who would independently verify that these systems are free of bias?”
  • “How would your proposal protect people wrongly flagged by a predictive system?”
  • “Does the delegate accept that data collected for security should not be reused for unrelated purposes?”
  • “How does the delegate propose that the Council, which cannot legislate, enforce this?”

Answering POIs

  • Pause. Answer the question you were asked, briefly.
  • “Thank you for that question. The United Kingdom believes…”
  • Do not know? Say so (see Challenges & Responses).

Typical flow of a session

  1. Roll call and setting the agenda.
  2. Speakers' list and formal debate.
  3. Moderated and unmoderated caucuses.
  4. Working papers become draft resolutions.
  5. Amendments, then voting.

Before committee

  • Read the rules of procedure and ask how points work.
  • Prepare a 1-minute opening speech and 3 key points.
  • Learn which countries are likely allies or opponents.
  • Bring a short clause bank (e.g. “Requests OHCHR to prepare a report on…”).

Moderated and unmoderated caucuses

  • Moderated: speak early on a narrow topic, make one clear point, link back to previous speakers.
  • Unmoderated: move quickly. Find 2–3 delegates, offer a concrete clause, collect signatories, share paper.

Speeches

Simple structure (about 60–90 seconds)

  1. Hook: a striking, true point.
  2. Position: the UK's view.
  3. Evidence: one law or dated fact.
  4. Solution: one or two concrete proposals.
  5. Close: a memorable line.
Sample opening speech (UK, HRC)
“Honourable Chair, distinguished delegates. A prediction is not proof. The United Kingdom recognises that surveillance technology can help protect the public from serious harm, but it must be lawful, necessary, proportionate and overseen. In December 2025 our Government launched a consultation on a clearer legal framework for facial recognition, because safeguards must keep pace with technology. We urge the Council to request an OHCHR report on best practice and to support technical assistance for States building safeguards. Security and human rights must be protected together. Thank you, Chair.”
Sample reply speech in a moderated caucus
“The United Kingdom thanks the delegate of… and shares the concern on bias. We would add that independent testing, before and after deployment, is the most practical safeguard. We invite delegates to support this in the working paper.”

Delivery tips

  • Slow down; look up, not at your notes.
  • One idea per sentence.
  • Use dated facts, not exaggerations.

Negotiation

Approaching delegates

  • “Hello, I'm the delegate of the United Kingdom. Could we talk about safeguards?”
  • Ask what their priority is first, then find overlap.
  • Bring a one-line proposal, not a lecture.

Building alliances

  • Likely overlap: States prioritising privacy, non-discrimination, and technical assistance.
  • Offer something: a shared clause, co-sponsorship, credit for their idea.
  • Do not assume a bloc's position: ask and listen.

Where the UK can compromise

  • Flexible: wording, timelines, reporting requirements, voluntary guidance.
  • Firm: no blanket ban on lawful, safeguarded security uses; mandate must respect national security.

Example negotiation lines

  • “Could we phrase this as ‘encourages’ rather than ‘demands’? That keeps within the Council's mandate.”
  • “If we add independent oversight, would your delegation co-sponsor?”

Challenges & Responses

“Why not ban surveillance completely?”
“The United Kingdom recognises that surveillance can help prevent serious crime and protect public safety. Our position is that it must be lawful, necessary, proportionate and independently overseen.”
“What if the algorithm identifies someone as dangerous?”
“A prediction is not proof. Important decisions must be based on reliable evidence and meaningful human review.”
“Does the United Kingdom have problems with facial recognition?”
“We recognise that the legal framework has been complex and that safeguards must keep pace with technology. That is why the Government consulted on a clearer framework in 2025–2026.”
“How can countries with fewer resources comply?”
“International cooperation, technical assistance, shared testing methods and training can help countries build safeguards without needing to develop everything independently.”
“The UK uses mass surveillance against its own citizens.”
“The United Kingdom takes that concern seriously. Our powers are set in law and subject to independent oversight, and we are consulting on clearer rules. We welcome specific suggestions.” (Stay factual; do not deny criticisms exist.)
“Your own courts found facial recognition use unlawful.”
“The Court of Appeal identified shortcomings in one force's policy in 2020. That is part of why the Government is seeking a clearer framework.”

Answering unexpected questions

  • Pause, restate the question, answer in one sentence, then bridge back to your key points.
  • Separate what you know from what you assume.

If you do not know

  • “I do not have that figure in front of me. The delegate may wish to consult the official source, and I will return to this in the caucus.”
  • “The United Kingdom's position is…, but I would not want to give an inaccurate number.”
  • Never invent a statistic. Honesty protects your credibility.
