Human Rights Council · United Kingdom · Agenda: Measures to Protect Women Migrant Domestic Workers from Forced Labour

UK Position

Core position: the United Kingdom condemns forced labour and domestic servitude and supports practical protection for women migrant domestic workers through criminal law, victim identification and support, fair recruitment and international cooperation with both origin and destination countries.
  • Zero tolerance for forced labour; traffickers and abusive employers must be prosecuted.
  • Victims should be identified and supported (a debate position: see criticisms below).
  • Prevent abuse at source: no recruitment fees charged to workers, clear contracts, accurate information before travel.
  • Work with the ILO, labour-sending States and civil society.
  • Favour practical cooperation and recommendations over binding new obligations imposed by the Council.

Useful distinction: these are sensible debate positions consistent with the UK's stated anti-slavery approach. Do not present every bullet as a quotation from UK policy.

Main arguments

1. Forced labour is a crime, not an immigration issue
The Modern Slavery Act 2015 criminalises slavery, servitude and forced or compulsory labour. Domestic servitude is a recognised form of exploitation.
2. Prevention beats rescue
Debt from recruitment fees, deception about the job and confiscated passports are the usual routes into forced labour. Fix recruitment and contracts first.
3. Workers need a safe way to complain
People will not report abuse if reporting risks losing their home, wages or immigration status. Support reporting channels, NGOs and hotlines.
4. Cooperate with sending and receiving States
Fair recruitment depends on both ends. Offer technical assistance, information sharing and training of labour inspectors.

Binding instruments

  • ICCPR (in force 1976): Art. 8 (slavery, servitude, forced labour), Art. 7 (cruel treatment), Art. 26 (non-discrimination).
  • ICESCR: Art. 7 (just and favourable conditions of work).
  • CEDAW (adopted 1979) and its General Recommendation No. 26 (2008) on women migrant workers.
  • ILO Forced Labour Convention, 1930 (C29): defines forced labour as work or service exacted under the menace of a penalty and not offered voluntarily.
  • ILO Domestic Workers Convention, 2011 (C189): adopted 16 June 2011, in force 5 September 2013. The UK has not ratified it (verify on NORMLEX).
  • Palermo Protocol (2000) on trafficking in persons; ECHR Art. 4.
Do not confuse: the 1990 UN Migrant Workers Convention is a separate treaty and the UK is not a party. UDHR is not legally binding; ICCPR, ICESCR and CEDAW are for States Parties.

Topic Explained

Migrant domestic worker
A person who travels to another country to work in a private household, for example as a cleaner, carer or cook. Most domestic workers worldwide are women.
Forced labour
Work exacted under the menace of a penalty and not offered voluntarily (ILO C29). Domestic servitude is forced labour inside a home.
ILO indicators of forced labour
Abuse of vulnerability, deception, restriction of movement, isolation, physical and sexual violence, intimidation and threats, retention of identity documents, withholding of wages, debt bondage, abusive working and living conditions, excessive overtime.
Tied visas and kafala-type systems
Systems where legal status depends on one employer. Leaving the employer can mean losing the right to stay, which makes complaints risky.
Recruitment fees and debt bondage
Agents may charge workers large fees, so workers arrive in debt and feel unable to leave.
Why it is hardWhat helps
  • Work is hidden inside private homes.
  • Isolation; language barriers.
  • Passports held by employers.
  • Fear of deportation.
  • Gaps in labour law coverage.
  • Fair recruitment, no fees for workers.
  • Written contracts in a language the worker understands.
  • Freedom to change employer.
  • Safe reporting channels, NGOs, hotlines.
  • Prosecution of traffickers.
Key argument: the person with the least power in the home needs the clearest legal route out of it.

Practical safeguards you can propose

  1. No recruitment fees charged to workers.
  2. Written contracts before departure.
  3. Right to keep documents.
  4. Freedom to change employer.
  5. Safe complaint routes separate from immigration enforcement.
  6. Labour inspection that respects privacy.
  7. Victim support: shelter, legal aid, recovery.
  8. Accountability for abusive employers and agencies.

What the Human Rights Council can do

  • 47 Member States.
  • Adopts resolutions and recommendations.
  • Can request OHCHR reports; works with Special Rapporteurs and the Universal Periodic Review.
  • Cannot force States to change domestic law.

UK Visa & Enforcement

How the system works (verify current rules)

  • Overseas Domestic Worker visa: allows a worker to come to the UK with an employer for a limited stay. Rules have changed since April 2012.
  • National Referral Mechanism: first responders refer potential victims; the Home Office decides on recognition; support is available to those referred.
  • Prosecution: offences under the Modern Slavery Act 2015.
  • Civil society: charities such as Kalayaan support migrant domestic workers.

Criticisms

  • Dependence on one employer can make workers afraid to leave.
  • Fear that reporting abuse could lead to immigration enforcement.
  • Changes in 2022 and 2023 criticised for reducing protection for some victims.
  • Domestic households are hard to inspect.
  • The UK has not ratified ILO C189.

Safeguards the UK can point to

  • Criminal offences and an Independent Anti-Slavery Commissioner.
  • A framework to identify and support victims.
  • Court findings (C.N. v United Kingdom, 2012) led to reform of the criminal law.

How to ask intelligent POIs

  • One sentence, a real question.
  • Target a gap: funding, enforcement, mandate, who pays.
  • Ask something that forces a commitment.
  • Stay polite.

Example POIs for this agenda

  • “Who would pay the recruitment fee under your clause, the employer or the worker?”
  • “How would a worker report abuse safely if complaining risks losing their immigration status?”
  • “How would inspectors check a private home without violating privacy?”
  • “Does the delegate accept that employer-tied visas increase the risk of forced labour?”
  • “The Council cannot legislate; how would this be monitored?”

Answering POIs

  • Pause, answer briefly, bridge back to your point.
  • “Thank you for that question. The United Kingdom believes…”
  • Do not know? Say so (see Challenges & Responses).

Typical flow of a session

  1. Roll call and setting the agenda.
  2. Speakers' list and formal debate.
  3. Moderated and unmoderated caucuses.
  4. Working papers become draft resolutions.
  5. Amendments, then voting.

Before committee

  • Read the rules of procedure and ask how points work.
  • Prepare a 1-minute opening speech and 3 key points.
  • Learn which countries are likely allies or opponents.
  • Bring a short clause bank (e.g. “Requests OHCHR to prepare a report on…”).

Moderated and unmoderated caucuses

  • Moderated: speak early on a narrow topic, make one clear point, link back to previous speakers.
  • Unmoderated: move quickly. Find 2–3 delegates, offer a concrete clause, collect signatories, share paper.

Speeches

Simple structure (about 60–90 seconds)

  1. Hook: a true, striking point.
  2. Position: the UK's view.
  3. Evidence: one law or dated fact.
  4. Solution: one or two proposals.
  5. Close: a memorable line.
Sample opening speech (UK, HRC)
“Honourable Chair, distinguished delegates. Behind a closed door, no one can see whether a worker is paid, free to leave, or safe. The United Kingdom condemns forced labour and domestic servitude in every form. Our Modern Slavery Act 2015 makes them crimes, but we recognise that criminal law alone is not enough. We urge the Council to support fair recruitment without fees charged to workers, safe ways to report abuse, and technical assistance for States building protections. We also invite the Council to request an OHCHR report on best practice. No one should work unprotected behind a closed door. Thank you, Chair.”
Sample reply speech in a moderated caucus
“The United Kingdom thanks the delegate of… and shares the concern about recruitment debt. We would add that workers also need a safe way to report abuse, without fear for their status. We invite delegates to support this in the working paper.”

Delivery tips

  • Slow down; look up.
  • One idea per sentence.
  • Use dated facts, not exaggeration.

Negotiation

Approaching delegates

  • “Hello, I'm the delegate of the United Kingdom. Could we talk about recruitment fees?”
  • Ask their priority first, then find overlap.
  • Bring a one-line proposal.

Building alliances

  • Labour-sending States may care about worker welfare and remittances; labour-receiving States about regulation and sovereignty. Ask, don't assume.
  • Many delegates will value ILO standards, women's rights and anti-trafficking language.
  • Offer a shared clause, co-sponsorship or credit for their idea.

Where the UK can compromise

  • Flexible: wording, timelines, voluntary guidance, reporting.
  • Careful: do not commit the UK to ratify C189 or new treaties without instruction; keep language on national immigration control (conference-dependent, check your background guide).

Example negotiation lines

  • “Could we say ‘encourages’ instead of ‘requires’? That stays within the Council's mandate.”
  • “If we add safe reporting channels, would your delegation co-sponsor?”

Challenges & Responses

“Why hasn't the UK ratified ILO Convention 189?”
“The United Kingdom takes protecting domestic workers seriously, and relies on its criminal law, including the Modern Slavery Act 2015, and its support for ILO standards. We welcome discussion on how all States can strengthen protections.” (Verify ratification status first.)
“Your visa traps workers with abusive employers.”
“We recognise the concerns raised by civil society and keep our rules under review. Workers who are victims can seek support through the National Referral Mechanism.” (Check current visa rules before adding details.)
“Your immigration laws put victims at risk.”
“The United Kingdom is committed to protecting victims while preventing abuse of the system. We welcome specific suggestions.”
“How can States inspect private homes?”
“Privacy matters, so inspection must be proportionate. Practical options include worker hotlines, NGO outreach and inspections based on complaints.”
“Why shouldn't domestic work be fully regulated like any other job?”
“We agree domestic workers deserve decent work. States can extend labour protections to them while balancing the privacy of the household.”
“How can States with limited resources comply?”
“Technical assistance, shared training for labour inspectors, and cooperation between sending and receiving States can help.”

Answering unexpected questions

  • Pause, restate the question, answer in one sentence, bridge back.
  • Separate what you know from what you assume.

If you do not know

  • “I do not have that figure in front of me. I will check the official source and return to it.”
  • Never invent a statistic.
